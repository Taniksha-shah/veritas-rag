The safety and efficacy of metformin on metabolic syndrome parameters in prostate cancer patients receiving androgen deprivation therapy: A systematic review and meta-analysis

The World Health Organization (WHO) states that prostate cancer is the third most common diagnosed cancer worldwide [1]. Prostate cancer patients are more likely to experience metabolic syndrome and its associated conditions [2]. Combination therapy can enhance results for cases with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer [3]. The standard treatment for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer is androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) plus an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor (ARPI) with or without docetaxel [3]. However, ADT monotherapy is frequently prescribed in older and frail patients. While ADT and ADT combination therapy are effective, they can lead to severe metabolic side effects such as weight gain, muscle or bone mass loss, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, and mortality [4–6].

Elevated insulin levels and body composition can increase mortality from prostate cancer-related and non-cancer diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease [7,8], reducing the effectiveness of ADT for PCa. After one year of ADT, weight increase ranged from 1.8 to 3.8%, with fat mass ranging from 9.4 to 23.8% [9,10]. Incidence of prostate cancer is higher in older patients and those with pre-existing diseases including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and renal impairment [11,12]. Furthermore, ARPIs are expensive and thus limiting their use in low- and middle-income nations. In these countries, the number of people diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer is predicted to rise significantly [13]. Cost-effective and safe therapeutic options for common diseases, like prostate cancer, are needed.

Metformin, a widely known antidiabetic agent, has been reported to improve survival for patients with diabetes or impaired fasting glucose while controlling hyperglycemia, weight gain, and macrovascular adverse events [14,15]. Furthermore, metformin has been shown in preclinical research to have both direct and indirect anticancer effects [16]. It has a low-cost and favorable safety profile that may be used to prevent metabolic problems of ADT while also improving prostate cancer outcomes.

The effectiveness of metformin in treating prostate cancer has been investigated for its potential in mitigating metabolic syndrome in this group. Previous studies on metformin’s dual role in prostate cancer patients receiving ADT has revealed benefits in cancer-specific outcomes and short-term metabolic outcome changes [17–19]. However, these were small-scale studies and conducted in a variety of settings, including monotherapy and combination therapy.

The STAMPEDE trial was a phase 3 clinical trial that included patients with locally advanced or metastatic cancer [20]. This trial found no meaningful evidence of an overall survival advantage from adding metformin to standard of therapy in patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. However, the metformin group reported considerably fewer unfavorable metabolic side effects with ADT than the standard care.

Based on these findings, pooled analysis of studies assessing the role of metformin in prostate cancer patients receiving ADT is warranted. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to assess the safety and efficacy of metformin on metabolic syndrome parameters associated with ADT in prostate cancer patients.

The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251241140). This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement standards [21] and is conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews [22]. PRISMA checklist is provided in supplementary appendix.

In November, 2025, we conducted a thorough search of the PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Scopus databases using the term (prostate cancer OR prostatic neoplasm) AND (androgen deprivation therapy OR ADT OR GnRH agonist OR castration) AND (metformin). Detailed search strategies are displayed in Table S-1.

To determine which papers satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria, two distinct authors independently evaluated the search results based on title and abstract of each study. We obtained full texts of potentially pertinent papers for additional evaluation after our first screening of abstracts and titles. In order to identify other potential studies that met our inclusion criteria, we manually searched for relevant articles. Throughout the screening process, disagreements were settled by consensus.

Study design: Randomized and non-randomized controlled trials were included.

Observational cohorts, case reports, reviews, and editorials were excluded. Also, we excluded studies that did not meet any other part of our inclusion criteria.

Data retrieved from included studies were gathered by two authors using an excel spreadsheet. We extracted data on the study’s design, blinding, country, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention, control, and follow-up duration. We also gathered baseline demographic and clinical information, including age, BMI, waist circumference, HbA1c, diabetes status, Gleason score, cancer stage, baseline PSA, prior ADT exposure, and ADT type. Regarding the extracted primary outcomes, these included HbA1c, body weight, waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, fasting triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and total cholesterol at multiple follow-up time points, in addition to adverse events.

Two authors used the Cochrane tool for RCTs (ROB2) [23] to evaluate the quality of the included studies. We employed ROBINS-I (‘Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions’) for evaluating risk of bias in non-randomized trials [24]. The Robvis web tool was used to generate quality assessment Figure 2 [25]. Any disagreements were settled through consensus.

We utilized R 4.5.0 [26] with R Studio 2024.12.1 + 563 [27] to conduct our analysis. We used risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals for dichotomous data while continuous data were analyzed using mean difference (MD) and 95% CI. In addition to I-squared (I2) and chi-squared (Chi2) statistics, the forest plot was visually examined to assess statistical heterogeneity between trials. I2 values of 50% showed high heterogeneity. All findings were examined using a random-effects model. We employed the Egger test [28] and funnel plot to examine publication bias and small-study effects where there were ten or more studies; otherwise, we used the DOI plot and LFK index [29]. We utilized the trim-and-fill method to estimate the number of missing studies and update the pooled estimates [30]. In order to formally identify significant outliers, we used Baujat plots [31].

Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) was used to reduce the likelihood of random errors caused by repeated significance tests in cumulative meta-analysis. A computer software program called Trial Sequential Analysis Viewer (TSA Viewer) was utilized (Version 0.9.5.10 Beta. Copenhagen: Copenhagen Trial Unit, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, Rigshospitalet. 2016) [32]). A two-sided design was utilized, with a type I error (α) of 0.05 and a type II error (β) of 0.10 (power of 90%). Both the alpha- and beta-spending functions were set to the empirical O’Brien-Fleming type (esOF). Futility boundaries were not used. The required information size (RIS) was calculated without regard for heterogeneity and then raised by 3% to accommodate for the sequential design. The sequential monitoring boundaries for benefit and harm were determined, and the cumulative Z-curve was plotted against these boundaries to see if firm evidence was obtained before reaching the RIS. The analysis was done retrospectively, and the study order was decided based on their appearance in the dataset.

We included ten clinical trials, comprising nine RCTs [19,20,34–40] and one prospective non-randomized trial [33]. The overall sample size comprised a total of 2642 adult patients with prostate cancer, with 1388 in the metformin groups and 1254 in the control groups. The mean age groups varied from 67 to 75 years. BMI averages ranged from 23.6 kg/m2 to 31.1 kg/m2. The baseline waist circumference had mean values ranging from 89 cm to 110 cm. Glycemic status at baseline varied from exclusively non-diabetic patients with mean HbA1c levels of roughly 5.6% to diabetics with HbA1c levels greater than 8.5%. The trim-and-fill – adjusted random-effects meta-analysis, which included five studies after imputing two potentially missing ones, demonstrated a significant overall reduction in the outcome favoring the intervention (MD = −1.15; 95% CI −1.89 to −0.41; p = 0.0023). Heterogeneity was minimal (I2 = 0%), and the Q-test indicated no significant between-study variability (p = 0.81), suggesting that the included studies were highly consistent. Although the trim-and-fill procedure added two studies with more negative effect estimates, the adjusted pooled effect remained significant and directionally stable, indicating that the observed benefit of the intervention is robust and unlikely to be explained by publication bias (Supplementary appendix).

This meta-analysis comprised ten clinical trials that compared metformin to placebo on a variety of metabolic outcomes in patients with prostate cancer undergoing ADT. Our findings showed that metformin had no significant advantage over placebo in terms of HbA1c, BMI, waist circumference, or body weight. Subgroup analyses of waist circumference revealed a substantial reduction at 8–9 and 18 months. We also found no significant difference in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, fasting triglycerides, or HDL cholesterol, with the exception of LDL and total cholesterol at 12-month follow-up. We also found a significant increase in the adverse event of diarrhea in the metformin group.

ADT is a crucial part of the treatment of metastatic and locally advanced prostate cancer [41–43]. Patients with prostate cancer now have far better results thanks to recent developments in hormonal treatment; however, this has resulted in prolonged exposures to ADT and the toxicities that go along with it. ADT toxicity is caused by unfavorable metabolic and anthropometric changes, resulting in exacerbation of MS symptoms and related consequences [6,8,44]. A recent study verified that patients had significant weight gain, increased abdominal obesity, and worsening glucose metabolism within nine months of beginning ADT [37]. These anthropometric and metabolic changes occur especially when taken for extended periods of time, as in the case of locally advanced prostate cancer. These alterations continued and were typically more noticeable after a longer period of time [37]. As patients live longer with novel systemic treatments, preventing adverse consequences becomes increasingly important in clinical practice.

Understanding the metabolic disorders related to ADT has led to recommendations that it should only be administered to men who are expected to benefit significantly from it and that the overall treatment duration should be maintained to a minimum as possible [45]. ASCO guidelines [46] recommend the use of ADT for 18 months based on findings of a main trial that failed to show survival benefit of 36-month over 18-month regimen duration [45]. Metformin has proved ability to lower hepatic gluconeogenesis which is probably due to the reduction in glucose and HbA1c [47]. AMPK-linked reduction in hepatic lipogenesis and cholesterol is probably responsible for metformin’s ability to manage lipid levels [48].

According to Usmani et al. [19], during the first year of treatment, males on ADT gained weight and developed abdominal obesity, consistent with available evidence [9]. Additionally, Usmani et al. showed that five months after starting ADT, metformin was able to reduce this weight gain and, to a lesser extent, increases in waist circumference [19]. At 9, 12, and 18 months, the PRIME trial found that the metformin arm had a lower HbA1c than the placebo arm [37]. Furthermore, at nine months, metformin was shown to significantly lower TC and show a positive trend toward lower LDL [37].

However, these alterations were temporary and did not persist with further follow-up, which are in accordance with our findings. Metformin could not mitigate these effects with prolonged follow-up, indicating that these alterations are unlikely to be clinically relevant in this patient population. In accordance with our findings, a previously published meta-analysis of the role of metformin in the same cohort as our study found no significant differences in body weight, HbA1c, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, or prostate-specific antigen [49]. While we subgrouped patients at different follow-up durations, identified differences were not maintained across lengthy follow-up durations in outcomes such as waist circumference, LDL, or total cholesterol.

Evidence has shown that after five years, 20% of diabetics on metformin alone lose glycemic control [50]. Even though earlier loss of response to metformin should be less common, even a small loss in effect could lower the difference in metabolic syndrome prevalence between the two study groups, decreasing the likelihood of a statistical benefit. Also, patients on ADT lead less active lives during therapy due to fatigue, depression, and other unfavorable side effects, which may lessen the likelihood of favorable and sustained changes in metformin outcomes. Since prior research examining the effects of similar lifestyle modifications in patients using metformin has demonstrated improved weight, abdominal circumference, and BMI after six months, other factors including food and exercise may also have had an impact on the study’s findings [39].

Nobes et al. compared metformin and controlled lifestyle interventions, including a low glycemic index diet and exercise regimen, to standard treatment during a 6-month period [39]. The STAMPEDE study 23 showed significant changes in body weight and metabolic markers (HbA1c, total cholesterol, and LDL) without observed differences in fasting triglycerides or HDL cholesterol levels [20]. The study found a 5.37 kg difference in favor of the intervention arm after 6 months of follow-up, indicating that metformin combined with lifestyle changes may have a greater impact than metformin alone [39]. Our study suggests that metformin may need to be combined with other methods, such as exercise or nutritional treatments, to produce clinically significant blunting of increases in mean weight more than 4 kg and sustain this change over a prolonged follow-up. The incidence of adverse events from metformin in our study was low, in accordance with previous randomized studies [37]. This confirms that metformin is safe and well tolerated among patients receiving ADT.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the most complete pooled analysis with the largest sample size to evaluate the effect of metformin on metabolic syndrome indices in prostate cancer patients undergoing ADT. We used rigorous analysis approaches such as TSA and trim-and-fill, as well as leave-one-out and Baujat plots in cases with heterogeneous results.

However, our findings must be interpreted with caution because the significant heterogeneity observed for crucial outcomes cannot be overlooked. Furthermore, TSA reports inconclusive evidence, indicating the necessity for future large-scale, randomized trials with the needed sample size.

According to our meta-analysis, metformin did not significantly enhance the major metabolic parameters of prostate cancer patients following ADT. Our findings add to existing evidence by integrating the STAMPEDE and PRIME trials, which indicated significant changes in metabolic markers, but the pooled analysis failed to show a benefit of metformin. As a result, the existing data does not support the routine use of metformin to treat ADT-induced metabolic syndrome. Metformin may need to be used in conjunction with dietary changes and supervised exercise, among other structured lifestyle treatments and this should be investigated in future large-scale randomized trials.

Our meta-analysis found that metformin had no significant effect on the metabolic parameters of prostate cancer patients undergoing ADT, such as HbA1c, BMI, waist circumference, or body weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and various lipid profile parameters. While it has a safe and well-tolerated profile, its efficacy for this particular use in the prostate cancer population is limited. Metformin’s clinical benefit in this instance appears to be low when administered alone. Hence, future trials should investigate the combination of medication like metformin and structured lifestyle interventions.

Ribociclib-Induced Vitiligo: A Case Report with Histological, Dermatoscopic, Confocal Microscopy, and LC-OCT Findings Along with a Narrative Literature Review

Cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors have emerged as a pivotal therapeutic strategy for the first-line treatment of metastatic estrogen receptor (ER)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer [1] and are also recognized as valuable treatments for patients with high-risk ER-positive early breast cancer [2]. The incorporation of CDK4/6 inhibitors—ribociclib, palbociclib, and abemaciclib—into endocrine therapy regimens has been associated with improved therapeutic outcomes in the majority of patients with advanced ER-positive breast cancer compared to endocrine therapy alone. The therapeutic effectiveness of this combination in hormone-sensitive breast cancer is primarily attributed to its inhibitory effects on estrogen-mediated intracellular pathways that regulate cell cycle progression [3,4]. Furthermore, preclinical evidence indicates that CDK4/6 inhibitors modify tumor cell biology through mechanisms that extend beyond the inhibition of cell cycle progression. These mechanisms include tumor cell survival, autophagy, senescence, cellular differentiation, DNA repair, cellular metabolism, and stress responses [5], in addition to enhancing antitumor immune responses [6].

This combination approach has led to substantial improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) while maintaining a favorable toxicity profile [7–12].

Cutaneous toxicities, while relatively infrequent within the overall adverse effect profile, constitute slightly over 10% of the total toxicities associated with CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy [13]. The most frequently reported dermatological events include alopecia, rash, and pruritus [14]. Rare dermatological events, such as iatrogenic vitiligo-like lesions, lupus erythematosus (LE), and toxic epidermal necrolysis, occur in less than 1% of cases recorded in real-world clinical practice [15]. Their incidence has been corroborated through pharmacovigilance databases, although only a limited number of case reports are available in the literature [16–22]. Both sporadic and iatrogenic forms of vitiligo lesions typically manifest as well-defined, depigmented macules with irregular borders, distributed asymmetrically across the skin, resulting from the selective destruction of melanocytes [23]. Conversely, CDK4/6 inhibitor-induced vitiligo-like lesions markedly differ from other iatrogenic forms of vitiligo (e.g., checkpoint inhibitor-induced) as well as from spontaneous vitiligo in terms of epidemiological distribution [24], etiopathogenesis [25–27], association with other autoimmune disorders [25], and relevant clinical history, including family or personal history of vitiligo. These lesions also display distinct characteristics regarding their site of onset [28,21], clinical progression [28], and the presence of accompanying symptoms such as prodromal signs [29]. Despite the potential adverse effects of CDK4/6 inhibitor-induced vitiligo-like lesions on patients’ quality of life, evidence from a case series indicates that the appearance of such lesions during treatment is associated with improved PFS. This phenomenon could potentially confer a survival benefit even when therapy is prematurely discontinued due to the progression of vitiligo [17]. These findings suggest an enhanced immune response induced by CDK4/6 inhibitors, as evidenced by immune cell infiltration observed through line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT).

In this report, a case of a female who developed vitiligo-like lesions during treatment with ribociclib in combination with aromatase inhibitors for metastatic, hormone-sensitive breast cancer was studied with the objective of investigating the characteristics of ribociclib-induced vitiligo.

Images were captured before and after a treatment period during which, based on imaging results, a topical corticosteroid regimen was administered to assess its effectiveness in halting vitiligo progression and promoting repigmentation [30–32]. The primary objective was to elucidate the underlying etiopathogenic mechanisms of these lesions using advanced dermatological techniques, facilitate appropriate treatment, and potentially reduce the risk of premature discontinuation of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy due to progressive vitiligo. Additionally, the study aimed to explore the implications of vitiligo onset on the prognostic profiles of breast cancer patients.

Furthermore, this study sought to elucidate the immunemediated mechanisms associated with ribociclib-induced vitiligo.

A 60-year-old postmenopausal female, lacking relevant comorbidities or a family history of breast and ovarian cancer, was diagnosed in 1996 with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. She underwent a quadrantectomy with axillary lymphadenectomy, followed by an integrated adjuvant therapy regimen that included chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and hormone therapy for the subsequent five years.

In 2022, after a disease-free interval of 26 years, the patient experienced a recurrence of the disease, presenting with diffuse visceral and bone metastases. Histology confirmed a Grade 3 invasive carcinoma of no special type (ER 90%, progesterone receptor [PgR] 70%, HER2 0, Ki67 28%). The patient tested negative for BRCA mutations. First-line treatment with ribociclib, administered orally at a full dose of 600 mg once daily for 21 days every four weeks, plus the nonsteroidal aromatase inhibitors letrozole, administered at a fixed continuous dose of 2.5 mg orally once daily, was initiated in August 2022, resulting in partial disease remission and an improvement in ECOG performance status, with no treatment-related toxicity observed until December 2023. At that time, the patient reported generalized pruritus and the development of diffuse cutaneous erythema. These symptoms prompted a reduction in the ribociclib dosage (400 mg/day, two 200-mg tablets daily for three weeks every four weeks) in combination with topical steroids, short-term oral low-dose corticosteroids (prednisone), and antihistamines, resulting in symptomatic relief. After 24 months of ribociclib treatment, asymptomatic areas of hypopigmentation appeared on the upper limbs and hands. A dermatological evaluation identified achromic patches, consistent with iatrogenic vitiligo (Figure 1A). The diagnosis was confirmed with a Wood’s lamp examination, reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) and LC-OCT. RCM was performed using the VivaScope 1500 system (MAVIG GmbH), and LC-OCT imaging was conducted upon the appearance of dyschromic lesions using the deepLive™ system (DAMAE Medical), which integrates optical coherence tomography (OCT) and reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) to simultaneously generate real-time vertical (B-scan) images. A histopathological examination of a skin biopsy specimen was conducted at the margin of a region exhibiting pigment loss. Immunostaining for MelanA was performed.

The patient was prescribed topical methylprednisolone aceponate, to be applied daily for pulsed courses of two weeks/month.

The same regions were imaged to observe changes in melanocyte distribution, inflammation, and epidermal pigmentation after five months of treatment (T1). A three-dimensional reconstruction of the affected areas was generated from en face image stacks to visualize spatial changes during the treatment period. Early therapy initiation enabled us to halt the progression of vitiligo-like lesions. As a consequence, the patient was able to continue ribociclib therapy with sustained oncological stability, maintaining the first dose reduction (400 mg/day). Standardized imaging conditions were maintained, with the patient seated in a fixed position. The patient remained progression-free at the last visit, achieving stable disease with minimal residual disease by July 2025.

Following the appearance of dyschromic lesions, dermatological examination identified achromic patches of hypopigmentation on the upper limbs and hands, consistent with iatrogenic vitiligo (Figure 1A). Wood’s lamp examination accentuated the skin depigmentation and revealed the characteristic, well-defined contrast that is pathognomonic for vitiligo lesions, aiding in distinguishing affected areas from normal skin and confirming the diagnosis (Figure 1B).

A reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) was performed for real-time assessment of the epidermis and upper dermis, as well as light coherence optical tomography (LC-OCT) [29], to evaluate the vitiligo-like lesions. RCM provided valuable insights into the microstructural alterations within vitiligo lesions, demonstrating that the normally visible bright, well-defined ring patterns, generated by the presence of melanocytes and melanin-containing keratinocytes at the dermoepidermal junction (DEJ), corresponding to the dermal papillae of healthy skin, were markedly reduced or absent. This loss or attenuation of the ring structures reflects the depletion and dysfunction of melanocytes, highlighting the cellular basis of the hypopigmentation characteristic of vitiligo. As a result, the DEJ appears blurred, with only faint outlines of the former papillary rings visible. Furthermore, the absence of bright, melanin-laden cells in the upper epidermal layers contributes to a generally diminished reflectance, aiding in the RCM-based differentiation of vitiligo-like lesions from normally pigmented skin. Furthermore, hyper-reflective inflammatory cells were observed in the superficial dermis (Figure 1D). The microstructural alterations align with the diagnosis of iatrogenic vitiligo, confirmed by LC-OCT imaging (Figures 1, 3A–B). LC-OCT imaging revealed a loss of definition of the DEJ, corresponding to the loss of structural integrity of the DEJ, altered melanocyte distribution, and the presence of inflammatory infiltrates.

Histopathological examination (Figure 2) of the affected areas revealed heterogeneity within the samples, with regions showing melanocyte depletion and others containing large melanocytes with dendritic morphology, as highlighted by immunohistochemical staining.

The analysis revealed a complete absence of melanocytes and melanin pigment in the vitiligo area (Figure 2A), highlighting the presence of lymphomononucleate cells at the DEJ, where they exhibited features of epidermotropism and a sparse, perivascular inflammatory infiltrate. Immunostaining for MelanA demonstrated a distinct difference between the area of pigment loss (Figure 2B) and the area of pigment retention, where relatively large melanocytes exhibiting dendritic morphology were identified (Figure 2C).

After five months of treatment (T1), the imaging of the same regions assessed therapeutic responses, including potential re-pigmentation and reduction of inflammation (Figures 1C–D).

Posttreatment RCM imaging (Figures 1, 2C) showed evidence of re-pigmentation, with pigmented, hyper-reflective keratinocytes, along with a decrease in inflammatory cell infiltration. Additionally, immunoreactive dendritic melanocytes are present in the basal layer, suggesting melanocyte repopulation and an effective therapeutic response (Figures 1, 2D).

LC-OCT assessments (Figures 1, 3C–D) indicate a reduction in lymphocyte numbers within the dermal papillary region, accompanied by an improved distribution of melanocytes and diminished inflammation. The integrity of the dermoepidermal junction appeared to be re-established, aligning with clinical signs of improvement and pigment restoration.

Vitiligo-like reactions induced by CDK4/6 inhibitors have been documented in a limited number of cases in the literature, and to our knowledge, no case has been studied using advanced noninvasive imaging techniques [10,14]. The emergence of well-defined, depigmented macules during ribociclib therapy, and even more rarely with abemaciclib or palbociclib, underscores that vitiligo-like reactions are extremely rare adverse events associated with this class of drugs. Conversely, vitiligo is a frequently reported immune-related adverse event (irAE) in patients undergoing anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy [33]. Pigmentary changes have also been reported in patients treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as imatinib, cabozantinib, and pazopanib [34].

Drug-induced vitiligo-like lesions likely involve distinct pathogenic mechanisms compared to sporadic vitiligo. While the exact pathogenesis of sporadic vitiligo remains incompletely understood, it is believed to arise from a complex interplay of oxidative stress, autoimmunity, and genetic factors [24] that contribute directly to the majority of melanocyte loss observed in this condition; however, it remains unclear whether this destruction is primarily driven by autoimmune responses or intrinsic cellular abnormalities [24,25]. The activation of the immune system, with the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, can lead to the destruction of melanocytes [24–26]. The central role of immune factors is further supported by the frequent occurrence of immune-mediated comorbidities, including other autoimmune disorders such as type 1 diabetes, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and rheumatoid arthritis [25] as well as other immune-mediated dermatoses like atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and lichen sclerosus, which are frequently associated with spontaneous vitiligo. Additionally, alterations in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) function appear to play an important role in the pathogenesis of the sporadic forms [24]. Conversely, inhibition of the p16INK4a/Cyclin D1-CDK4/6/RB pathway by CDK4/6 inhibitors, crucial to melanocyte development and pigmentation, may contribute to druginduced vitiligo-like lesions [21]. Notably, this form of vitiligo appears to be independent of the autoimmune processes typical of sporadic and drug-induced vitiligo, instead resulting from drug-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in melanocytes. This unintended on-target mechanism likely involves impaired proliferation of keratinocyte precursors and subsequent apoptosis, leading to the loss of survival signals, premature melanocyte death, and hypopigmentation [15]. Simultaneously, although these inhibitors do not directly target the immune system, they may indirectly modulate immune responses, potentially inducing vitiligo-like skin toxicity through immunological mechanisms.

CDK4/6 inhibitors may elicit an immune response against shared antigens present in both normal melanocytes and breast cancer cells. This cross-reactivity could lead to permanent toxicity while simultaneously aiding in the control of metastatic disease [35,36]. These processes may act individually or synergistically, contributing to the development of depigmented lesions characteristic of vitiligo in patients treated with CDK 4/6 inhibitors (Table 1).

Reports of vitiligo-like toxicities emerging during ribociclib therapy suggest that, although rare, they may have clinical significance due to their underlying immunological mechanisms and consequent potential prognostic implications [17]. Rashighi et al. reported an aberrant immune response against melanocytes related to an altered immune microenvironment induced by CDK 4/6 inhibitors, characterized by the activation of cytotoxic T cells and other immune cells, such as macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) subpopulations [37]. This response may also be indicated by the peculiar onset and progression of dyschromic lesions described in the literature. Specifically, vitiligo-like skin depigmentation was observed early during treatment with ribociclib, and the depigmentation persisted even after switching from the CDK 4/6 inhibitor [17]. Notably, these patients experienced prolonged PFS, suggesting a potential association between skin toxicity and favorable treatment outcomes [22]. All these observations support the concept that, in addition to inducing cell cycle arrest, treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors also activates antitumor immunity [35].

Interestingly, data from in vitro studies, animal models, and breast cancer patients indicate that CDK4/6 inhibitors enhance antitumor immunity by improving tumor antigen presentation and depleting the population of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), a subset of CD4+ T cells, through the inhibition of their proliferation and promotion of differentiation [38]. Additionally, these inhibitors simultaneously increase interleukin-2 (IL-2) secretion by effector T cells and promote T cell activation. These mechanisms counteract tumor immune evasion and enhance tumor immunogenicity [39,40]. This finding is significant because Tregs play a crucial role in suppressing immune responses and maintaining homeostasis, thereby enhancing immunological tolerance within the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, Tregs contribute to tumor development and progression by collaborating with tumor cells to inhibit anti-cancer immune responses. They employ several mechanisms, including blocking dendritic cell (DC)-derived costimulatory signals (CD80/CD86) via cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA4), consuming IL-2 through high CD25 expression and directly inducing the apoptosis of effector T cells [41]. The significant downregulation of the effector Treg subset, along with the overall Treg population, induced by CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy, appeared to be independent of the specific CDK4/6i molecule administered. This downregulation was significantly associated with therapeutic response and patient outcomes, as reported by Scirocchi et al. [42]. Concurrent with this, CDK4/6 inhibitors increase PD-L1 protein levels, facilitating enhanced tumor evasion (Figure 3).

These findings are corroborated by noninvasive imaging such as RCM and LC-OCT to monitor the onset of vitiligo during CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment, resulting from potential systemic activation of the immune system leading to melanocyte destruction.

Additionally, several elements indirectly support the possibility of systemic immune activation in the case we report. Firstly, the case involved a relatively young female with no family or personal history of vitiligo or autoimmune diseases. Additionally, vitiligo manifested after an extended period of treatment. Notably, the dyschromic lesions were preceded by a prolonged period of generalized pruritus and erythema, which occurred several months before the onset of vitiligo. It is important to highlight that generalized pruritus is classified as an irAE in clinical trials of immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors [43]. Furthermore, the pruritus responded to a low dosage of corticosteroids, and this symptom is absent in non-iatrogenic forms of vitiligo. Skin biopsy confirmed the loss of melanocytes typical of vitiligo, along with aggregated lymphocyte clusters. Finally, our case aligns with previous observations suggesting that vitiligo itself may be considered an irAE, indicating that the local inflammatory and immune reaction could reflect a systemic activation of the immune system. This finding is further supported by the duration of the therapeutic response we observed, with a PFS still not reached at approximately 34 months, exceeding the median PFS of 25.3 months reported in the phase 3 MONALEESA-2 [44,45].

In conclusion, both LC-OCT and RCM are valuable tools in the noninvasive clinical assessment and management of drug-induced vitiligo-like lesions. While RCM has been more extensively utilized, LC-OCT offers significant advantages, such as its ability to visualize deeper skin structures and track disease progression, making it a promising addition for improving diagnostic accuracy and guiding treatment decisions. The superior capacity of LC-OCT to visualize deeper skin layers is particularly valuable for monitoring the progression of vitiligo and assessing treatment efficacy. Moreover, its high-resolution and three-dimensional imaging capabilities facilitate detailed observation of melanocyte activity and epidermal changes, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy [46,47]. This is crucial for halting vitiligo progression and promoting repigmentation of affected areas while preventing the discontinuation of effective cancer treatments due to skin toxicity [48]. Moreover, the predictive significance of vitiligo occurrence during CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment, resulting from potential systemic activation of the immune system leading to melanocyte destruction, provides a unique opportunity to deepen our understanding of the complex interactions between CDK4/6 inhibitors and tumor cell biology. This interaction extends beyond cell cycle inhibition and impacts patient prognosis. Furthermore, it enriches our understanding of the mechanisms underlying iatrogenic vitiligo.

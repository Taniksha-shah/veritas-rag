Fenfluramine-associated pulmonary hypertension: a study of adverse events reporting system FAERS

To the Editor:

Fenfluramine is an amphetamine derivative first available in in France and the US as an appetite suppressant in 1963 and 1973, respectively. Fenfluramine is a chiral molecule and has been marketed as a racemate (Pondimin) or a d-enantiomer, dexfenfluramine (Isomeride, Redux). Fenfluramine and its active metabolite, norfenfluramine, are 5-HT1B, 5-HT2C and 5-HT2B receptor agonists, increasing serotonergic transmission. Cardiovascular toxicity of fenfluramine is thought to result from excessive serotonergic stimulation, notably through activation of 5-HT2B receptors [1]. After a worldwide use of 30 to 40 years and 50 million patients exposed, both fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine were withdrawn from the US and French markets in 1997 due to serious cardiovascular adverse effects. Indeed, the International Primary Pulmonary Hypertension Study notably demonstrated an increased risk of primary pulmonary hypertension (PH), now known as idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), and was followed by reports of valvular heart disease in fenfluramine users [2–4]. A third, related drug, benfluorex, which shares norfenfluramine as a metabolite, remained available for several years in France but was finally withdrawn in 2009 for the same reasons [5, 6].

Yet, a new racemic-fenfluramine formulation (Fintepla) was repurposed in the treatment of Dravet syndrome (DS) and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) and have received marketing authorisation by the US Food and Drugs Agency (FDA) and the European Medical Agency in 2020. Both are genetic diseases that cause a rare, severe and resistant form of epilepsy, and can lead to neuro-cognitive deficiency or even death. Sodium valproate is the first-line treatment, and other common anti-epileptic drugs are recommended as alternatives (e.g. clobazam or lamotrigine). However, in some serious resistant forms, fenfluramine can be used as an adjuvant [7]. To mitigate the risk of cardiovascular adverse events, the drug is only available through restricted programmes with mandatory echocardiogram assessments before, during (every 6 months), and after treatment. During the clinical development programme and post-commercialisation safety study, among 374 patients no case of PH nor persistent valvular heart disease was reported, for a median (interquartile range (IQR)) exposure time of 824 (7–1280) days [8]. Several arguments have been put forward to explain the seeming reduction of cardiovascular adverse events in this population, notably smaller sample size, differences in patient age, dosing regimens (six to seven times lower than the doses used in the appetite suppressant indication) and underlying comorbidities [1]. Nevertheless, a case of a 4-year-old child presenting with increased right pulmonary pressure 6 months after the introduction of Fintepla was published in 2024, leading to the modification of the labelling of the FDA black box warning and emphasising the importance to study the risk of cardiovascular adverse effects with fenfluramine used in epilepsy [9].

In order to characterise and provide insights about fenfluramine-associated PH, we synthesised all cases reported in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database, using a pre-defined collection of MedDRA Standardised Medical Query terms: cor pulmonale, cor pulmonale acute, cor pulmonale chronic, PAH, pulmonary artery pressure abnormal, pulmonary artery pressure increased, pulmonary artery wedge pressure increased, pulmonary artery dilatation, pulmonary artery wall hypertrophy, PH, pulmonary hypertensive crisis, pulmonary valve incompetence, pulmonary vascular resistance abnormality, vascular resistance pulmonary increased, pulmonary veno-occlusion disease [10]. Moreover, through the US Freedom of Information Act, we requested access to the narratives of cases reporting Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension with Fintepla.

As of 30 June 2025, 2984 individual case safety reports associated with Fintepla were reported in the FAERS database. Of these cases, 150 reported adverse events of interest: 110 (73.3%) cases of pulmonary valve incompetence; 20 (13.3%) cases of pulmonary arterial pressure increase; 15 (10.0%) of PAH; 11 (7.3%) of PH; 3 (2.0%) of pulmonary artery dilatation; 1 of (0.7%) pulmonary vascular resistance abnormality; and 1 (0.7%) pulmonary arterial pressure abnormal. 60 patients (40.0%) were female, 89 (59.9%) were male and 1 (0.7%) was not reported. The number of cases increased over years from eight cases in 2022, to 72 cases in 2024 and 44 in 2025 (up to June). At the time of the adverse event, 64 (42.7%) patients were under 18 years old, 60 (40.0%) were between 18 and 64 years old, and 26 (17.3%) were not reported. Overall, 149 (99.3%) cases were considered serious, and Fintepla was considered the only suspected drug in most cases (144, 96.0%).

15 cases reported the MedDRA term PAH, even though no confirmation by right heart catheterisation was mentioned. These 15 echocardiographic PH cases were reported for 10 adults (8 LGS, 2 DS) and five children (3 LGS, 2 DS). In the available data, eight patients had a baseline normal echocardiogram. Patients' characteristics and timelines between Fintepla initiation and PH are summarised in figure 1. The median (IQR) time to onset of an increase in pulmonary pressure was 363 (335–624) days, with an average number of echocardiograms of 1.24 every 6 months for children and a median (IQR) time of 380 (193–826) days, and an average number of echocardiograms of 0.95 every 6 months for adults. 12 cases (80%) also reported mild or moderate valvular dysfunction, as regurgitation or structural modifications, and three (20%) echocardiographic PH cases improved after drug discontinuation. For all the other cases, information on the evolution, after drug continuation or dose modification, was unknown and no rechallenge have been performed. In this analysis of FAERS, we found that the number of reports of PH with the use of Fintepla is steadily increasing in both children and adults. This is likely due to a combination of an increase in the number of patients exposed, a longer duration of exposure, and possibly increased reporting. However, due to the lack of data on population exposure and the unknown rate of reporting, pharmacovigilance databases do not allow the risk or incidence of adverse events to be quantified. Moreover, details of echocardiographic findings were often missing in cases, notably after drug withdrawal, and no right heart catheterisation results were reported (or performed) despite the coding of PAH by the reporters, impairing the causal assessment of fenfluramine role. Furthermore, we cannot exclude that some unreported confounding factors such as other cardiac comorbidities, multiple exposure to drugs, and repetition of hypoxia episodes during seizure may have contributed to PH. These results highlight the importance of comprehensive descriptions of clinical, biological and haemodynamic findings in case narratives and accurate coding of symptoms in pharmacovigilance databases.

We also observed considerable heterogeneity in fenfluramine dose and time-to-onset of PH, ranging from few months to several years after starting treatment, suggesting a poor dose- and time-dependence of PH in susceptible individuals. For the only three patients with documented follow-up, echocardiography showed a return to normal of pulmonary pressures. Improvement of valvular heart diseases was indeed observed in patients exposed to fenfluramine as an appetite suppressant [12]. However, in the largest case series of fenfluramine-associated PAH, the median duration of fenfluramine-derivatives intake was 6 months, with a median time between exposure and PAH diagnosis of 4.5 years [13]. In these patients the clinical course of the disease was similar to idiopathic PAH. Regular echocardiographic monitoring is therefore likely to be effective in detecting early valvular heart disease, which is more likely to regress. However, the prevention of PAH development remains to be evaluated, particularly by following these patients after discontinuation of drug treatment. Yet, in a report based on the French National Healthcare Database, around 20% of patients did not have a cardiac follow-up after the treatment introduction [14].

These results confirm the plausibility of valvulopathy and PH onset in patients exposed to fenfluramine used in the treatment of epilepsy, reinforce the importance of careful echocardiographic monitoring, and highlight the need for a better understanding of the risk factors, progression, and potential sequelae of fenfluramine-associated PH, particularly in children.
